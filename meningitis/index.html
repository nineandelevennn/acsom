USMLE Step 1 Anatomical Associations:
  • Meningitis involves inflammation of the Leptomeninges (Arachnoid + Pia mater).
  • CSF circulates in the Subarachnoid Space (between arachnoid and pia).
  • Arachnoid granulations reabsorb CSF into the superior sagittal sinus; bacterial exudate obstructs these granulations leading to communicating hydrocephalus.

⚡ 4. Pathophysiology: "Why Does This Symptom Occur?"

Active Recall: Predict the physiological mechanism before clicking to reveal!

Question: Why do patients develop Nuchal Rigidity, Kernig, and Brudzinski signs?

Mechanism: Inflammation in the subarachnoid space irritates meningeal nociceptors. Stretching inflamed spinal nerve roots (flexing the neck or extending the knee with hip flexed) increases tension on the hypersensitive meninges, triggering severe pain and involuntary protective muscle spasm (rigidity).

Question: What causes Cerebral Edema & Elevated ICP in Bacterial Meningitis?

Triple Edema Mechanism:

  1. Vasogenic Edema: Inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1) disrupt blood-brain barrier (BBB) tight junctions.
  2. Cytotoxic Edema: Neutrophil degranulation and bacterial toxins cause cell death and neuronal swelling.
  3. Interstitial Edema: Purulent exudate blocks arachnoid villi, impairing CSF reabsorption (communicating hydrocephalus).

🩺 5. Clinical Presentation & Physical Signs

The classic triad of acute bacterial meningitis consists of: Fever, Nuchal Rigidity, and Altered Mental Status (present in ~44% of patients; >95% have at least 2 of: fever, headache, neck stiffness, altered mental status).

Physical Sign Elicitation Technique Positive Response
Kernig Sign Flex hip and knee to 90°, then passively extend the knee. Resistance or pain in lower back/hamstrings.
Brudzinski Sign Passively flex the patient's neck in supine position. Involuntary flexion of hips and knees.
Jolt Accentuation Ask patient to rotate head horizontally 2-3 times per second. Worsening of baseline headache.

⚖️ 6. Differential Diagnosis: Meningitis vs Encephalitis

Distinguishing brain parenchymal involvement from leptomeningeal inflammation is critical:

Feature Meningitis Encephalitis
Primary Tissue Affected Leptomeninges & Subarachnoid Space Brain Parenchyma
Cerebral Function Preserved early (headache, stiff neck, photophobia) Prominently Abnormal: Confusion, altered personality, focal deficits, seizures
Classic Etiology S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, Enteroviruses HSV-1 (temporal lobe focus), Arboviruses (WNV)

📐 9. Clinical Decision Protocol: LP vs Head CT

🚨 GOLDEN RULE: NEVER delay empiric antibiotic therapy for diagnostic procedures! If head CT is required prior to LP, blood cultures must be drawn and empiric antibiotics/dexamethasone administered IMMEDIATELY prior to transport to CT.
Step 1: Patient suspected of acute bacterial meningitis. Does the patient have high-risk criteria for herniation?

High-risk criteria: Focal neurological deficits, papilledema, altered consciousness/coma, new-onset seizures, severe immunocompromise.

USMLE High-Yield Fact: Dexamethasone Co-administration

Administer IV dexamethasone prior to or concurrently with the first dose of antibiotics in pneumococcal meningitis to decrease neurological sequelae (especially sensorineural hearing loss caused by inner ear inflammatory damage).

⚠️ 12. Meningitis Complications

Sensorineural Hearing Loss

Most common long-term complication of pneumococcal meningitis. Caused by inflammatory damage to cochlear hair cells.

Waterhouse-Friderichsen

Adrenal hemorrhage secondary to N. meningitidis endotoxemia/DIC → acute adrenal failure and refractory shock.

Communicating Hydrocephalus

Inflammatory purulent exudate permanently scarring or plugging arachnoid granulations impairing CSF reabsorption.
